## From a trick to a method

The last chapter found a kernel for our loop by hand, by staring at the
chain of loads, multiplies and adds until a pattern appeared. A compiler
cannot stare; it needs a method that works for any loop and any machine,
including machines that can start several instructions per cycle, each
on its own kind of unit.

The method is to give **every trip the same schedule**. We choose a time
t(x) for each instruction x, measured from the start of its own trip, and
start a new trip every II cycles (II is the initiation interval from the
last chapter). Trip i then runs instruction x at cycle i × II + t(x).
That one decision turns a problem about endless overlapping trips into a
problem about a small table, as we are about to see.

<Analogy title="a bus timetable">
A bus leaves the depot every ten minutes, and every bus follows the same
timetable: the high street 4 minutes after leaving, the station 13
minutes after. Two buses are at the station at the same moment only if
their timetables put them there at times that differ by a multiple of
ten; so to keep the stops from crowding, the planner only needs to check
the timetable "minutes past each ten".
<span slot="breaks">Buses can be late and bunch up. A modulo schedule is
exact: every trip keeps to its times, cycle for cycle, which is why
checking one table of II rows is enough.</span>
</Analogy>

<Analogy title="a rota that repeats every week">
A café plans one week of shifts and repeats it all year. If the
espresso machine can only be cleaned by one person at a time, the rota
must not put two cleanings on the same weekday, even if they belong to
"different weeks" of some long project.
<span slot="breaks">A rota is for people, who can swap shifts. The
reservation table is for hardware units, which have no slack at all: a
second load in the same row of a one-load machine is simply
impossible.</span>
</Analogy>

## The modulo reservation table

Two instructions of different trips run in the same cycle exactly when
their times are equal modulo II (they leave the same remainder when
divided by II). So the whole loop's use of the machine is described by a
**modulo reservation table**: II rows, one column per kind of unit, and an
instruction at time t occupies row t mod II of its unit's column. If no
two instructions share a cell, no cycle of the running loop ever asks a
unit to do two things at once.

From that table we can read off a lower bound before we schedule anything.
If a unit is used u times per trip and there are c of them, it has c × II
cells, so II must be at least u ÷ c, rounded up: the **resource bound**,
ResMII. On our one-instruction-per-cycle toy machine, five instructions
need five cycles: ResMII = 5, the kernel of the last chapter.

The other bound comes from **recurrences**: values that one trip passes
to the next, like the running sum. A loop-carried edge (chapter 216 called
them carried dependences) has a delay d and a **distance** δ, the number
of trips it reaches across. If a chain of such edges comes back to where
it started, the value goes round it once every δ trips and needs d cycles
to do so, so II must be at least d ÷ δ, rounded up: the **recurrence
bound**, RecMII.

<Formal title="Modulo scheduling">

```text
trip i runs x at cycle i × II + t(x)
x uses row t(x) mod II of its unit
edge a → b, delay d, distance δ:
    t(b) ≥ t(a) + d − δ × II
ResMII = max over units ⌈uses ÷ count⌉
RecMII = max over cycles ⌈Σ d ÷ Σ δ⌉
MII = max(ResMII, RecMII)
```

<span slot="read">Trip i runs instruction x at i times II plus x's time.
x takes row t of x mod II in its unit's column. For an edge from a to b
with delay d that reaches δ trips ahead, b's time must be at least a's
time plus d, minus δ times II (because b's trip starts δ × II cycles
later). The resource bound is the largest, over units, of uses divided by
how many there are, rounded up; the recurrence bound is the largest, over
cycles of edges, of total delay divided by total distance, rounded up; and
the minimum initiation interval is the larger of the two.</span>
</Formal>

## Filling the table

Now a slightly wider toy machine, closer to a real core (LLVM's model of
SiFive's 7-series cores gives memory instructions and multiplies separate
pipes<Cite id="llvm-sifive7-sched" />):
in each cycle it can start one load, one multiply
and one ALU instruction, and branches use the ALU. Our loop has three ALU
instructions, so ResMII = 3; its recurrences need only 1; MII = 3. We
start at II = 3 and place the instructions in order of height, each at
the earliest time its scheduled predecessors allow, sliding later one
cycle at a time (at most II − 1 times) until its cell is free:

<MrtSteps client:visible />

<Callout kind="definition">
**Modulo scheduling** finds a software-pipelined loop by giving every
trip the same schedule t, starting one trip every II cycles. It computes
the minimum initiation interval MII = max(ResMII, RecMII), then tries to
place every instruction in a **modulo reservation table** of II rows
while meeting every dependence, raising II by one whenever it fails. The
stage of an instruction is t ÷ II rounded down, and the kernel, prologue
and epilogue are built from those stages.
</Callout>

What if an instruction's whole window, all II rows, is full, or the only
free rows break a dependence with something already placed? A plain
greedy scheduler gives up and tries II + 1. Rau's **iterative** modulo
scheduling does better: it places the instruction anyway and **evicts**
whatever it collides with, which goes back on the list to be placed again;
a budget limits how much evicting it may do at one II before it gives up
and moves to II + 1<Cite id="rau1994" />. That small amount of
backtracking is what lets it reach MII on real loops for machines with
many kinds of units. Production compilers follow the same outline: LLVM's
pipeliner, for example, starts from the computed MII and, if the
instructions cannot be scheduled within it, increases it by one and tries
again<Cite id="llvm-machinepipeliner" />.


## What you learned

- **Modulo scheduling** gives every trip the same schedule and starts one
  every II cycles, so resource clashes reduce to a **modulo reservation
  table** with II rows.
- **ResMII** comes from how often each unit is used; **RecMII** from
  values carried round loops; **MII** is the larger.
- Instructions are placed by height at their earliest legal time; if
  that fails, II goes up by one. Rau's **iterative** version evicts and
  retries within a budget first.

Next: once instructions are scheduled, little patterns of waste are often
left behind; a small window sliding over the code can clean them up.
