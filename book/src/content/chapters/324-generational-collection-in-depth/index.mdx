## Two promises that pull apart

The chapter "NaN-boxing and generational collection" showed that most
objects die young, and split the heap in two; the chapter
"Mark-compact and the write barrier" showed V8's barrier recording
old-to-new pointers. This chapter makes that design exact. A young
collection makes two promises at once:

1. **It is fast**: it never looks at the old generation, which may be
   hundreds of times bigger than the young one.
2. **It is correct**: it keeps every young object that anything can
   still reach, including through a pointer stored in an old object.

The second seems to need the old generation after all. The way out is
to make every pointer *store* help: if the program tells the collector
when it creates an old-to-young pointer, the collector can find those
pointers without searching for them. Henry Lieberman and Carl Hewitt
proposed collecting by age in 1983, so that short-lived objects cost
less than long-lived ones<Cite id="lieberman1983" />, and David Ungar's
*Generation Scavenging* (1984) built the version that most collectors
since have followed: copy the young, and keep a **remembered set** of
old-to-new references<Cite id="ungar1984" />.

## The nursery and growing up

<Analogy title="a hospital's maternity ward">
Every baby is born on one small ward, which is cleaned constantly;
most visitors there come and go within hours. Those who stay long
enough are moved to the children's ward, and eventually leave for
ordinary life, where nobody checks on them daily.
<span slot="breaks">Babies are moved because of their age alone. An
object is moved because it *survived*: its age counts collections
lived through, not time.</span>
</Analogy>

<Analogy extra title="a probation period at work">
A new hire is reviewed every few weeks; after enough good reviews they
are made permanent, and reviewed once a year.
<span slot="breaks">Being made permanent cannot be undone in the
collectors in this chapter: an object that dies just after promotion stays in the old
generation, wasting space, until the next full collection.</span>
</Analogy>

The **nursery** is where new objects are born. It is small, it is
filled by handing out the next free address, and it is emptied by
copying, as in the previous chapter. Many collectors keep an object in
the young generation for a few collections before promoting it, so
that objects that were merely *recently* made, not long-lived, get a
chance to die. HotSpot, the JVM in OpenJDK, splits its young
generation into an *eden* and survivor spaces, counts each object's
age in collections survived, and promotes it at the **tenuring
threshold**, at most 15 by default; `SurvivorRatio` is the "ratio of
eden/survivor space size", 8 by default<Cite id="hotspot-gc-flags" />.
V8 keeps one extra step, the intermediate part<Cite id="v8-trash-talk" />.

<Figure takeaway="Objects are born in the nursery and must survive several young collections before they are promoted." caption="A generational heap. New objects go into the nursery; survivors move to a survivor space and age by one at each young collection; at the threshold they are promoted to the old generation.">
  <NurseryFigure />
</Figure>

<Callout kind="definition">
The **nursery** (or *eden*) is the part of the young generation where
new objects are allocated. A young (**minor**) collection copies its
live objects out, either into a survivor space, where each object's
**age** (collections survived) goes up by one, or, once the age reaches
the **tenuring threshold**, into the old generation (**promotion**).
</Callout>

<Examples of="nursery">

```text
Pause Young (Allocation Failure)
  35M->1M(123M) 2.152ms
```

```text
Age table with threshold 15
- age   1:     475560 bytes
- age   2:     419328 bytes
```

```text
--scavenger-max-new-space-capacity-mb
  default: 8
```

</Examples>

All three are real, from this book's machine. The first is OpenJDK 21's
Serial collector (`-Xlog:gc`) on a loop that makes 50 million small
records and keeps one in a hundred: a young pause took the heap from
35 MB to 1 MB in about 2 ms, because almost the whole nursery was dead.
The second is the same run with `-Xlog:gc+age=trace` after its second
young collection: 475,560 bytes have survived once and 419,328 twice,
and with a threshold of 15 they stay young for now. The third is from
`node --v8-options` (Node v22.23.4): the largest new space the
Scavenger will use is 8 MB (twice that without pointer compression,
the help text adds), tiny beside a heap of gigabytes.

## Remembered sets

A minor collection starts from the ordinary roots (stack, globals) and
from every old-to-young pointer. The collector cannot search the old
generation for those, so the program must record them as it makes
them, and the write barrier is where that happens. The record is the
**remembered set**.

<Analogy extra title="a guest list at the door">
A party in a small back room only lets in people on the list; the
list was written as invitations went out, so nobody has to search the
whole building on the night.
<span slot="breaks">A guest list is written once; a remembered set is
updated on every pointer store, and an entry can go stale when the old
object later drops its pointer, which costs a little wasted scanning
but is never unsafe.</span>
</Analogy>

Ungar's scavenger kept such a remembered set of old-to-new references,
filled as stores happened, and treated it as part of its
roots<Cite id="ungar1984" />. The runnable version below records the
old *objects*, which is the simplest form:

<Run id="remset" />

<Callout kind="definition">
A **remembered set** records, for a collection of one part of the heap,
the places outside that part that may hold pointers into it; for a
minor collection, the old objects (or old fields) that may point at
young objects. It is filled by the write barrier, and its entries are
extra roots. It may hold too much (stale entries), never too little.
</Callout>

<Formal title="What the remembered set must cover">

```text
∀ o ∈ Old, y ∈ Young :
  o → y  ⇒  o ∈ RS
```

<span slot="read">For every old object o and young object y: if o
points at y, then o is in the remembered set. The arrow only goes one
way, so the set may contain old objects that no longer point at
anything young; that is wasted work, not a bug.</span>
</Formal>

<Examples of="remembered-set">

```js
// old.kid = young: owner old, value
// young, so the barrier records old
```

```js
// young.parent = old: no record;
// the minor GC scans young objects anyway
```

```js
// old.kid = young; old.kid = null;
// old stays in the set: stale, but safe
```

</Examples>

The first is the one store that matters. The second needs no record,
because every live young object is scanned during the copy anyway, and
the old object it points at is not being collected. The third shows
why the arrow in the formula goes only one way: the barrier sees the
second store too, but removing entries would need the barrier to know
whether `old` holds *other* young pointers, so collectors leave the
entry and drop it at the next collection instead.

## Card tables

A set of objects needs a lookup on every recorded store. A **card
table** is cheaper still: divide the old generation into **cards** of a
fixed size and keep one byte per card; the barrier marks the byte for
the card being written as *dirty*, and asks no questions. HotSpot's
card-table barrier is exactly two steps after a reference store: find
the card's byte, at `base[addr >> card_shift]`, and store the dirty
value there<Cite id="hotspot-card-table" />. Cards are 512 bytes by
default, so `card_shift` is 9, and *dirty* is 0 while *clean* is
all ones<Cite id="hotspot-card-table" />.

```cpp
// HotSpot, after a reference store
CardValue* byte = _card_table
  ->byte_for(field);  // addr >> 9
*byte = CardTable::dirty_card_val();
```

(Wrapped to fit.) At a minor collection, the collector scans the old
objects on dirty cards only, treats their young pointers as roots, and
cleans the cards it has dealt with.

<Analogy extra title="sticky notes on a filing cabinet">
Instead of a list of every changed letter, you stick a note on the
drawer whenever you touch anything inside it. Later you check only the
drawers with notes, and look through each of them.
<span slot="breaks">A drawer is a few files; a card is 512 bytes of
whatever happens to sit there, so the collector must also find where
each object on a dirty card begins, which HotSpot keeps a separate
table for.</span>
</Analogy>

<CardSteps client:visible />

<Callout kind="definition">
A **card table** is an array with one byte per **card** (a fixed-size
block of the heap, 512 bytes in HotSpot). The write barrier marks the
card containing the written field as **dirty**. A minor collection
scans the objects on dirty cards for pointers into the young
generation, then cleans the cards. It is a remembered set that is
cheap to update and coarse to read.
</Callout>

<Formal title="Finding a card">

```text
card(a) = ⌊a / 512⌋ = a >> 9
```

<span slot="read">The card for address a is a divided by 512, rounded
down, which, because 512 is two to the ninth, is a shifted right by
nine bits (the chapter "Bits and bytes").</span>
</Formal>

<Examples of="card-table">

```text
store into 0x1234 → card 0x1234 >> 9 = 9
store into 0x13FF → card 9 again
store into 0x1400 → card 10
```

```cpp
// G1: skip the slow path for young cards
if (*byte != g1_young_card_val())
  write_ref_field_post_slow(byte);
```

```text
.NET: "Cards are set by JIT helpers
during assignment operations"
```

</Examples>

The first works the formula: 0x1234 is 4,660, and 4,660 / 512 is 9
and a bit; the last address of card 9 is 0x13FF, and 0x1400 starts
card 10. The second is OpenJDK 21's G1 collector (trimmed), which adds a filter:
a store into a young object needs no card at all, so the barrier
checks for the young card value first<Cite id="hotspot-card-table" />.
The third is .NET's design document: its JIT's store helpers set the
card when the stored object is in the young ("ephemeral") range, and
a young collection then looks only at objects on set
cards<Cite id="dotnet-gc-botr" />.

<Prompts ids={['ct-what', 'ct-shift', 'ct-tradeoff']} />

<Callout kind="engine" label="Under the hood">
V8 keeps its remembered sets per page rather than in one card table,
and its developers found that they are usually most of a scavenge's
roots<Cite id="v8-orinoco-scavenger" />. All three designs make the
same trade: a few instructions on every pointer store, in exchange
for never reading the old generation during a young collection.
</Callout>

## What you learned

- The **nursery** is where objects are born; survivors age, and are
  **promoted** at the tenuring threshold (15 by default in HotSpot).
- A **remembered set** holds the old places that may point at young
  objects; the write barrier fills it, and it may hold too much, never
  too little.
- A **card table** keeps one byte per card; the barrier dirties a card
  with a shift and a store, and a minor collection scans only dirty
  cards.
