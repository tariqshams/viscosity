## SSA for a finished CFG

You met SSA and phis in *SSA and phi nodes, built on the fly*, and the
dominator tree in *Dominators and natural loops*; this chapter goes
further, to the other way of building SSA, the one most textbooks teach
and LLVM uses: convert a **finished** CFG in which variables are
assigned many times. The method, from Cytron, Ferrante, Rosen, Wegman
and Zadeck<Cite id="cytron1991" />, has two steps: **place** the phis,
then **rename** every assignment and use. A phi for every variable at
every join would be correct, but most of those would choose between
identical values; the classic method puts a phi only where two
different versions can really meet, and the tool that finds those
places is the dominance frontier.

Here, as a reminder, is the dominator tree of the Collatz loop, which
every example below uses (b1 is the loop test, b2 the even-or-odd test,
b5 and b6 the two branches, b7 their join, b3 the jump back to b1 and
b4 the exit):

## Where a block's territory ends

The blocks a block dominates form its subtree, its territory: any value
it defines is the only version of that variable that can arrive
anywhere inside, unless the territory defines a newer one. The
interesting places are where the territory *ends*: the first blocks you
can step into from inside it that it does not own outright.

<Analogy title="border towns">
Each assignment rules a territory, and inside it everyone uses its
coins. Travellers from the territory only meet travellers carrying other
coins at the border towns just outside, so those are the only places
that need a money changer (the phi).
<span slot="breaks">A money changer is a lasting business; once a phi
is placed it counts as a new assignment with a territory of its own, so
it can make further border towns need changers, which is why the
placement is repeated until nothing new appears.</span>
</Analogy>

<Analogy extra title="ripples in a pond">
Drop a stone where `v` is assigned and its ripple spreads outward;
where two ripples from different stones meet, the water is mixed, and
that line is where a phi goes.
<span slot="breaks">Ripples spread in every direction and fade with
distance; a version spreads only forwards along edges, never fades, and
the "meeting line" is exactly the dominance frontier, not wherever
the waves happen to be strongest.</span>
</Analogy>

<Figure takeaway="DF(B) is where B’s territory meets the rest of the graph." caption="b2 dominates the shaded blocks. The edge b3 → b1 leaves the region, landing on a block b2 does not strictly dominate, so DF(b2) = {b1}.">
  <Frontier />
</Figure>

<Callout kind="definition">
The **dominance frontier** of a block X, DF(X), is the set of blocks Y
such that X dominates a predecessor of Y but does not strictly dominate
Y itself<Cite id="cytron1991" />. They are the joins where a path that
has passed through X meets a path that may not have.
</Callout>

<Formal title="The dominance frontier">

```text
DF(X) = { Y : X dom P for some
          P ∈ pred(Y),
          and not X sdom Y }
```

<span slot="read">The dominance frontier of X is the set of blocks Y for
which some predecessor P of Y is dominated by X, while X does not
strictly dominate Y. Y may be X itself: a loop header that dominates
the end of its own loop is in its own frontier.</span>
</Formal>

For the Collatz loop: DF(b5) = DF(b6) = \{b7\}, because each branch
dominates itself, a predecessor of the join, but not the join. DF(b2),
DF(b7) and DF(b3) are all \{b1\}, since each dominates b3, the source of
the back edge. DF(b1) = \{b1\}: the loop header dominates b3, which leads
back to b1, and no block strictly dominates itself. The entry and the
exit have empty frontiers.

Computing the frontiers is short once you have the tree. Only a block Y
with two or more predecessors can be in anyone's frontier; for each
predecessor P of Y, climb the tree from P, adding Y to the frontier of
every block you pass, and stop when you reach idom(Y). Every block on
that climb dominates P (it is P or above P), and none of them strictly
dominates Y, because Y's strict dominators are idom(Y) and the blocks
above it. (Cytron and colleagues compute the same sets bottom-up over
the tree<Cite id="cytron1991" />; the climb is Cooper, Harvey and
Kennedy's simpler version<Cite id="coopertorczon2022" />.)

## Where phis are needed

A variable `v` needs a phi at a block when two different assignments of
`v` can reach it along different paths. Inside an assigning block's
territory, its version is the only one that can arrive; it first has
to compete with another at the territory's frontier. However, a phi is
itself an assignment of `v`, so placing one can make another necessary,
at the phi's own frontier. The set of blocks that need a phi is
therefore the frontier of the assigning blocks, plus the frontier of
those, and so on until nothing new is added: the **iterated dominance
frontier**, DF⁺.

<Formal title="The iterated dominance frontier">

```text
DF(S)     = ⋃ DF(X), X ∈ S
DF₁       = DF(S)
DFᵢ₊₁     = DF(S ∪ DFᵢ)
phis(v)   = DF⁺(defs(v)), the limit
```

<span slot="read">For a set of blocks S, DF(S) is all their frontiers
put together (⋃ reads "the union over"). Start with DF₁, the frontier
of S; then repeatedly take the frontier of S together with what you have
so far. The sets only grow, so they stop changing; the final set is
DF⁺(S). The blocks that need a phi for v are DF⁺ of the blocks that
assign v<Cite id="cytron1991" />.</span>
</Formal>

The paper computes DF⁺ with a worklist: put every block that assigns
`v` on it; take blocks off one at a time; give each block in its
frontier a phi for `v` (once), and since a phi is an assignment, put
that block on the worklist too, if it has never been there. Step

<Callout kind="definition">
**SSA construction** turns a program whose variables are assigned many
times into SSA form. The classic method (Cytron et al.) works on a
finished CFG in two steps: it **places** a phi for each variable `v` at
every block of the iterated dominance frontier of the blocks that
assign `v`, then **renames** by walking the dominator tree with a stack
of versions per variable, so that every assignment and every phi defines
a new name and every use reads the version on top of its stack.
</Callout>

<Examples of="ssa-construction">

```text
x assigned in b0 only
DF(b0) = {}   →  no phis

diamond b0 → b1, b2 → b3
x assigned in b0 and b1
DF(b1) = {b3}  →  phi at b3

n assigned in b0, b5, b6
DF⁺ = {b7, b1}  →  phis at b7, b1
```

</Examples>

A variable assigned once, in the entry, needs no phi anywhere, because
the entry dominates every block and its frontier is empty. The diamond
is the one that surprises people: `x` is assigned before the `if` and
again only in the *left* branch, yet the join b3 still needs a phi,
`x3 = φ(x2, x1)`, because the right-hand path brings the old value
(which is why the paper treats the entry as assigning every variable:
there is always an old value to meet). The third is the Collatz loop
you stepped through: b7 comes from the branches and then b1 from b7's
phi, which is exactly where VIR has its two phis for `n`.

## Renaming with a stack

With the phis placed, every assignment (phis included) gets a fresh
version, and every use must read the right one. In straight-line code a
table of latest versions is enough; with branches, the latest version
depends on where you are. The trick is to walk the **dominator tree**,
because the versions that can reach a block are exactly those made in
it and in its ancestors in the tree.

<Analogy extra title="sticky notes in a house tour">
Touring a house room by room, you stick a note on a board each time
something is renamed in a room, and peel that room's notes off as you
leave it; the note on top always says what the name means where you are
standing.
<span slot="breaks">A house tour walks the corridors; the renaming
walks the dominator tree, which can jump from a room to one it is not
directly connected to, because "dominates" is not the same as "is next
to".</span>
</Analogy>

Keep one stack per variable, starting with version 0 (the value on
entry). In each block, in order: a phi or assignment of `v` pushes a new
version; a use of `v` reads the top. Then, for each CFG successor that
has a phi for `v`, fill in the operand that belongs to this block with
the top of the stack. Then visit this block's children in the dominator
tree, and on the way out pop every version this block pushed. Step

`n4 = φ(n2, n3)` at the join. The stack gets two things right without
any extra work: b6 reads `n1`, not b5's `n2`, because b5's push was
popped before the walk moved to its sibling; and the back edge from b3
fills b1's phi with `n4`, because b3 sits under b7 in the tree, so `n4`
is on top when the walk is in b3. Here is the walk as a program, for

<Callout kind="pitfall">
Cytron's placement is *minimal*, not perfect: it puts a phi wherever two
versions meet, even if nothing reads the variable after the join. Such a
phi is harmless but useless. LLVM's `mem2reg` places phis "using
dominator frontiers" and then rewrites loads and stores "in depth-first
order", which it calls "the standard SSA construction algorithm",
building the *pruned* form that leaves those useless phis
out<Cite id="llvm-passes" />.
</Callout>

## What you learned

- The **dominance frontier** DF(X) holds the blocks just outside X's
  territory: X dominates one of their predecessors but not them. Climb
  from each predecessor of a join up to the join's idom to compute it.
- A variable needs a phi where two of its versions can meet; a phi is an
  assignment too, so the frontier is **iterated**, and a worklist
  computes DF⁺.
- **Renaming** walks the dominator tree with a stack of versions per
  variable: push on assignment, read the top on use, fill successors'
  phi operands, pop on the way out.
- Placement plus renaming is **SSA construction** in the classic style
  of Cytron et al.; LLVM's `mem2reg` builds the pruned version.
