## The numbers, as they came out

Part IV began with a promise, in the chapter "Why bytecode?": flatten
the tree into a compact list of instructions, run them in one tight
loop, and the interpreter gets faster. The companion's benchmark script
runs ten Drip programs on both engines, each in a fresh process, and
keeps the best of three runs. Here is the most recent result:

<VmVsTreeChart />

The best the VM manages is 1.3 times faster (`method_calls`), and on
four programs (`fields`, `instantiation`, `strings` and `trees`) it is
*slower* than the tree-walker. That is not the textbook result, and it
is worth saying plainly why, because the reasons are about where the
program runs, not about bytecode being a bad idea.

## Why so little

**Both engines are JavaScript, and V8 compiles both.** In C, a
tree-walker pays for chasing pointers between scattered nodes and for
deciding what each node is, and a bytecode loop removes most of that.
Here, both engines are JavaScript programs run by V8, which watches
which functions are hot and compiles them to machine code with its
optimising tiers<Cite id="v8-maglev" />. The tree-walker's
`evaluate` methods get that treatment as well as the VM's `run` loop,
so the tree-walker is far from the slow, naive interpreter the
argument assumed. On the simplest loop there is, three million passes
of `for i in 0..3000000 { s += i; }`, the tree-walker was faster in my
runs: its best was 653 ms against the VM's 814 ms.

<Run id="time-loop" />

That is the same loop, a tenth as long, so you can time it on your own
device; `clock()` is in seconds, and your numbers will differ from mine.

**Every instruction is a trip round a loop written in JavaScript.** A
bytecode interpreter pays for decoding each instruction and jumping to
its code, every time, however simple the instruction<Cite id="v8-sparkplug" />.
That loop of three million passes runs 39,000,015 instructions in the
companion's VM (its `instructionsExecuted` counter says so), which at
814 ms is about 21 nanoseconds per instruction, around forty clock
cycles on this 2.1 GHz machine, for work like "push slot 1". Most of
that is the loop around the work, not the work: counting the
instruction, reading the opcode, choosing the `case`, and reaching the
stack and `sp` through the VM object.

**The VM rebuilds in JavaScript what the tree-walker borrows from
V8.** The tree-walker stores fields and variables in JavaScript `Map`s,
which are hash tables V8 implements in its own fast, built-in code; the
VM stores them in its own `Table`, written in JavaScript. The
tree-walker's objects are freed by V8 alone; the VM's are also tracked
on its object list and swept by its own collector, on top of V8's. That
is why the four slow programs are the ones about fields and allocation.
One measurement backs it up: a copy of the VM with instance fields in a
JavaScript `Map` ran `instantiation` 1.20 times faster than the real
one, and `fields` 1.09 times faster.

<Analogy title="a fluent reader and a phrasebook">
Suppose you hand a fluent reader a novel, and alongside it a version
you have rewritten in a compact shorthand of your own, with a key. For
a beginner, the shorthand might be quicker; for someone who already
reads fast, looking up each symbol in the key costs more than the
shorthand saves.
<span slot="breaks">Shorthand is a one-way loss of meaning; bytecode
loses nothing, and on a host without a JIT (or in C) the same VM would
beat the same tree-walker comfortably.</span>
</Analogy>

## What measurably helps

So I measured the changes themselves. Each row below is two versions
of the companion, identical except for one change, run alternately nine
times each in fresh processes, on the same shared machine; the bar is
the slower version's best time divided by the faster one's.

<ExperimentsChart />

The biggest by far is a deletion. When the heap frees an object, it can
**poison** it (the chapter "Reclaiming memory: mark and sweep"), which
deletes every property; V8 handles objects whose properties are
deleted by switching them to slower "dictionary" properties<Cite id="v8-fast-properties" />,
and doing that to every dead object is expensive. The repository's
history records that profiling first found this, when poisoning was
always on, and that the fix was to run it only in stress and debug
mode. Forcing it back on now makes `instantiation` 2.03 times slower and
`trees` 1.43 times slower. The lesson: profile before you guess, because
nobody would have guessed a safety net was the bottleneck.

Next comes `invoke` (the chapter "Invoking methods, and inheritance
in the VM"):
without it, `method_calls` is 1.32 times slower. Then `inc.local`,
which this chapter is about: 1.15 times on the plain loop, and 1.12 on
`sieve` by best times, though the medians for `sieve` were equal, so
that one is within the noise. And one change I expected to matter did
not: removing the instruction counter that `run` increments on every
instruction gave 1.03 times on `fib`, which is noise on a shared
machine.

## Superinstructions

`inc.local` and `invoke` have something in common: each does, in one
instruction, the work that would otherwise take several. Since every
instruction pays its trip round the dispatch loop, fewer, bigger
instructions mean fewer trips. Step through one pass of that loop to
see where `inc.local` sits:

<DispatchSteps client:visible />

<Analogy extra title="a set menu">
Ordering a starter, a main and a dessert one at a time means three
trips to the kitchen and three waits; the set menu is one order and one
trip, for the combination people choose most often.
<span slot="breaks">You can change your mind halfway through a meal;
the VM must make sure no jump ever lands in the middle of a fused
sequence, because there is no middle any more.</span>
</Analogy>

<Analogy extra title="a rubber address stamp">
A business that writes the same five-line address hundreds of times a
day buys a stamp: one press does the work of five lines of handwriting.
<span slot="breaks">A stamp prints one fixed address; a
superinstruction still takes operands (`inc.local 1` names its slot),
so it fits every use of the pattern, not one particular use.</span>
</Analogy>

<Callout kind="definition">
A **superinstruction** is a single opcode that does the work of a common
sequence of several, chosen by measuring which sequences run most
often. It saves dispatches and pushes and pops. Its costs are more
opcodes in the dispatch loop, a compiler that recognises the pattern,
and the rule that no jump may target the middle of the sequence.
</Callout>

<Formal title="What fusing saves">

```text
T ≈ N × (d + w)
fuse k instructions, run f times:
  N' = N − (k − 1) × f
```

<span slot="read">Running time T is roughly the number of instructions
run, N, times the cost of a dispatch d plus the instruction's own work
w. Fusing a sequence of k instructions that runs f times removes k
minus 1 dispatches each time, so N shrinks by (k − 1) × f, while the
work w stays about the same.</span>
</Formal>

<Examples of="superinstruction">

```text
inc.local 1
# = load.local 1, const 1, add,
#   store.local 1, pop
```

```text
invoke 'm' (1 args)
# = get.prop 'm', then call 1
```

```text
LOAD_FAST_LOAD_FAST
# CPython: two LOAD_FAST in one
```

</Examples>

The first is the companion's own: the compiler emits it for the hidden
counter of every `for` loop, replacing five instructions with one, so
k is 5 and f is the number of passes; for the three-million-pass loop
that is twelve million dispatches saved, exactly the difference between
51,000,015 and 39,000,015. The second is `invoke`, which fuses a
property read and a call and, as a bonus, skips an allocation. The
third is from CPython, whose compiler fuses two adjacent `LOAD_FAST`
instructions into one, but only inside a basic block (a straight run
of code that jumps enter only at the top) and only when both operands
are small enough to share one argument<Cite id="cpython-superinstructions" />.

## What you learned

- On this host the VM is at most **1.3×** faster than the tree-walker,
  and slower on four programs. V8 compiles both engines, each
  instruction costs about 20 ns of JavaScript, and the VM rebuilds hash
  tables and garbage collection that the tree-walker borrows from V8.
- Measured, one change at a time: poisoning off (up to 2×), `invoke`
  (1.3×), `inc.local` (small), fields in a `Map` (small), the
  instruction counter (nothing measurable).
- A **superinstruction** fuses a common sequence into one opcode,
  saving (k − 1) dispatches per use; `inc.local` and `invoke` are two.

## Every value in eight bytes

The companion's `Value` is `null | boolean | number | Obj`, and V8
decides how each of those is laid out in memory (the chapter "How
JavaScript stores numbers and objects"). A VM written in C has to
decide for itself. The obvious layout is the tagged value of the
chapter "Tagging values at run time": a tag saying what kind of value
it is, plus an 8-byte payload, which a C compiler usually pads to 16
bytes. Every stack slot, every field and every list element then costs
16 bytes, of which most of the tag's 8 are wasted.

**NaN-boxing** fits every value into one 8-byte double instead. In IEEE
754 (the chapter "Floats and typed arrays: numbers as bytes"), a double
whose eleven exponent bits are all ones and whose fraction is not zero
is a NaN, "not a number". That is a huge family of bit patterns
(about 2⁵³ of them, counting both signs), and ordinary arithmetic only ever produces one or
two. So a VM can pick a NaN pattern that arithmetic never makes (here,
the quiet-NaN bits plus one extra "box" bit), and decide that any
double with those bits set is not a number at all, but a boxed value
whose other bits carry a tag and a payload.

<Analogy title="a stamp on an envelope">
Every value travels in the same 8-byte envelope. Nearly every envelope
holds a number; however, one stamp, "not a number", is almost never
needed for arithmetic, so once a VM sees that stamp it reads the rest of
the envelope as "what kind of thing, and which one".
<span slot="breaks">A real envelope keeps its contents separate from
the stamp; in a double, the stamp *is* some of the bits, so the space
left for the contents is smaller (here 16 bits of tag and 32 of
payload).</span>
</Analogy>

<Analogy extra title="phone numbers for services">
Almost every phone number belongs to a person, but a short range is
kept back for services, and a number in that range is read as "which
service" rather than "which person".
<span slot="breaks">A phone network keeps the range free by agreement;
a NaN-boxing VM relies on arithmetic never producing its chosen
patterns, so it must make sure every real NaN it stores looks the
same.</span>
</Analogy>

<NanBoxSteps client:visible />

In JavaScript you can try this with two views over one buffer: a
`Float64Array` to read and write doubles, and a `Uint32Array` over the
same bytes to reach their bits. On a little-endian machine (almost
every machine today), the high word of slot `i` is `u32[2 * i + 1]`
and the low word `u32[2 * i]`:

<Run id="nan-box" />

Two catches. First, a real NaN must never look boxed, so a NaN-boxing
VM stores every NaN in one **canonical** form; that matters in
JavaScript too, because when a NaN is written into a typed array the
specification lets the engine choose its bit pattern<Cite id="ecma262" />,
so a NaN carrying "boxed" bits could survive the trip and be misread.
Second, JavaScript cannot put a pointer inside a double, so in this
experiment an object's payload is an index into an array of objects; in
C the spare bits can hold the pointer itself.

<Examples of="nan-boxing">

```text
1.5      hi 3ff80000  lo 00000000
none     hi 7ffc0001  lo 00000000
obj #7   hi 7ffc0004  lo 00000007
```

```text
is it a number?
  (hi & 0x7ffc0000) !== 0x7ffc0000
```

```text
NaN, as stored: hi 7ff80000 lo 0
(the box bit is clear: still a number)
```

</Examples>

The first shows three values as their two words: `1.5` is an ordinary
double, while `none` and object number 7 both have the box pattern
`7ffc` at the top, with tags 1 and 4 in the bottom of the high word and
the object's index 7 in the low word. The second is the whole type test
for "is this a number?": one AND and one compare, the cheapest check a
VM can make. The third is the canonical NaN: its exponent is all ones
and its quiet bit is set, but the box bit is clear, so it still reads
as a number.

<Callout kind="definition">
**NaN-boxing** represents every value as one 64-bit IEEE 754 double.
Numbers are stored as themselves; every other value is stored as a NaN
bit pattern that arithmetic never produces, with a tag (none, boolean,
object and so on) and a payload (a pointer or an index) in its fraction
bits. Telling a number from anything else is one mask and compare, and
every real NaN must be stored in one canonical form.
</Callout>

<Formal title="The two words of a boxed value">

```text
value = hi : lo        (32 + 32 bits)
number(v)  ⇔  hi ∧ BOX ≠ BOX
tag(v)     =  hi ∧ FFFF
payload(v) =  lo
```

<span slot="read">A value is a high word and a low word. It is a number
exactly when its high word, masked with BOX, is not BOX itself;
otherwise its tag is the low sixteen bits of the high word, and its
payload is the whole low word.</span>
</Formal>

## Most objects die young

The collector of the chapter "Tracing and sweeping: white, grey and
black" marks every live object on every collection. Is that wasteful?
I counted, for this chapter, how many of the objects present at each
collection survived it:

```text
program        collections  survived
instantiation           27      0.1%
closures                 4      0.1%
sort                     4      8.5%
trees                   10       54%
```

In three of the four programs, nearly everything the collector looked
at was already dead: the 600,000 points and segments `instantiation`
makes are garbage almost as soon as they exist. That is the
**generational hypothesis**, the observation that most objects die
young, and production collectors are built around it<Cite id="v8-trash-talk" />.
`trees` is the honest exception: it keeps big trees alive across
collections, so each full collection re-marks them.

<Analogy extra title="an inbox and a filing cabinet">
You clear your inbox every day, because most of what arrives there is
junk you can bin at a glance; the filing cabinet, where the few
important letters end up, you sort through once a year.
<span slot="breaks">You can see that a letter is junk; a collector can
only tell that an object is dead by finding that nothing reaches it,
and an old letter in the cabinet can still point at something in
today's inbox.</span>
</Analogy>

So split the heap in two. New objects start in the **young**
generation. A *minor* collection looks only at the young generation,
which is small and mostly garbage, so it is quick; objects that survive
a few minor collections are promoted to the **old** generation, which
is collected rarely.

<Figure caption="A generational heap: a minor collection traces only the young side, starting from the roots and the remembered set." takeaway="An old object pointing at a young one must be remembered, or the minor collection would free a live object.">
  <GenerationsFigure />
</Figure>

The catch is the picture's arrow: a reference from an old object to a
young one. A minor collection does not trace the old generation, so a
young object kept alive *only* by an old one would look dead. The fix
is a **write barrier**: a small check on every store of a reference
into an object, which records an old object that now points at a young
one in a **remembered set**, and the remembered set joins the roots
for the next minor collection. In the companion, the stores that would
need it are `set.prop` (a field), `index.set` and the native `push` (a
list element), `store.upvalue` on a closed upvalue, and `method` and
`inherit` (a class's method table); globals and the stack are roots
already. A sketch, not in the companion:

```ts
// a sketch: not in the companion
function barrier(owner: Obj, v: Value) {
  if (owner.old && isObj(v) && !v.old) {
    remembered.add(owner); // a root
  }
}
```

<Examples of="generational-gc">

```text
for i in 0..200000 {
  let s = [i, i];   # dies young
}
```

```text
let ps = [];       # survives, gets old
push(ps, [1, 2]);  # old -> young: barrier
```

```text
class O {}
let o = new O();
o.f = 3;   # a number: no barrier needed
```

</Examples>

The first is the shape of `instantiation`'s loop: every list is
garbage by the next pass, so a minor collection would find almost nothing to keep,
exactly the 0.1% above. In the second, `ps` lives for the whole program
and would be promoted, and pushing a new list into it creates an
old-to-young reference that the barrier must record, or the next minor
collection would free `[1, 2]`. The third shows the barrier's cheap
case: storing a number creates no reference at all, which is why the
check starts with `isObj(v)`.

<Callout kind="definition">
A **generational garbage collector** divides the heap by age. It
collects the young generation often and cheaply, promotes the objects
that survive, and collects the old generation rarely. Because a minor
collection skips the old generation, it needs a write barrier that
records old-to-young references in a remembered set, which serves as
extra roots.
</Callout>

<Prompts ids={['gen-hypothesis', 'write-barrier', 'trees-exception', 'number-store']} />

<Callout kind="engine" label="Under the hood">
These are the moves real engines make. SpiderMonkey, Firefox's engine,
stores JavaScript values NaN-boxed: on 64-bit machines it packs a tag
into the bits next to the exponent and a pointer into the rest, and
canonicalises NaNs before storing them<Cite id="spidermonkey-value" />.
V8's heap is generational, with a young generation (split into nursery
and intermediate parts) and an old one, a fast collector for the young
generation that copies survivors, and write barriers that keep track of
old-to-new references<Cite id="v8-trash-talk" />; those remembered
sets, kept per page of memory, are usually most of a minor collection's
roots<Cite id="v8-orinoco-scavenger" />.
</Callout>

## What you learned

- **NaN-boxing** keeps every value in one double: numbers as
  themselves, everything else in NaN patterns arithmetic never makes,
  with a tag and a payload. One mask tells numbers apart, and real NaNs
  must be canonical.
- The **generational hypothesis**, most objects die young, held for
  three of four companion programs measured; `trees` kept half.
- A **generational collector** collects the young often and the old
  rarely, and needs a **write barrier** to remember old-to-young
  references as extra roots.
