## Deciding too early

Every parser so far has worked from the top down. It starts with the
start symbol and, at each nonterminal, picks a rule by peeking at one
token, before it has seen any of the tokens that rule will cover. That
early choice is the source of all the trouble in the last two chapters:
`expr → expr "-" item` had to be rewritten, because a parser that
picks it first must then parse an `expr` with the same token still
next, forever; and the rewritten grammar leaned the wrong way, so the
parser had to rebuild the tree as it went.

What if the parser did not decide until it had *seen* a rule's whole
body? It would read tokens, keep them on one side, and the moment the
last few made a complete body, fold them into that rule's head. Small
pieces would become bigger pieces, until the whole input became the
start symbol. That is **bottom-up parsing**.

<Analogy title="flat-pack furniture">
You don't start by holding up a finished wardrobe; you bolt two side
panels to a shelf, then add the back, then hang the doors on the frame.
Each step joins pieces you already have into one bigger piece, and the
whole wardrobe exists only at the very end.
<span slot="breaks">Instructions tell you the order; a parser has no
instructions and must work out from the tokens which pieces to join
next, which is the hard part (and the next chapter's subject).</span>
</Analogy>

<Analogy title="laying down sets in a card game">
In rummy you draw cards into your hand one at a time, and whenever the
cards you hold make a complete set, you lay them down on the table as
one meld.
<span slot="breaks">A player can hold cards in any order and pick a
set from anywhere in the hand; a parser may only fold the cards it
picked up *last*, at the top of its stack.</span>
</Analogy>

## Shift and reduce

The common way to do it uses a stack and only two moves. A **shift**
moves the next token from the input onto the top of the stack. A
**reduce** finds a rule's body on top of the stack, pops it, and
pushes the rule's head in its place, which builds one tree node with
the popped pieces as its children. When the input is used up and the
stack holds only the start symbol, the parser **accepts**. Here is a
left-recursive grammar for subtraction, used exactly as written:

```text
E → E "-" T | T
T → NUMBER
```

<Callout kind="definition">
A **shift-reduce** parser keeps a stack of grammar symbols and repeats
two moves: **shift** the next token onto the stack, or **reduce** by a
rule `A → β` whose body β is on top of the stack, replacing β with
`A`. It accepts when the input is used up and the stack holds only the
start symbol.
</Callout>

<Formal title="The moves, as rewrites of (stack, input)">

```text
shift:   ( α , a w )  ⊢  ( α a , w )
reduce:  ( α β , w )  ⊢  ( α A , w )
                     if A → β
accept:  ( S , $ )
```

<span slot="read">A pair (alpha, w) is the stack, alpha, written with
its top at the right, and the input still to read, w. The turnstile ⊢
reads "moves to, in one step". Shift: if the input starts with the
token a, the parser can move a onto the stack. Reduce: if the top of
the stack is beta and A is made of beta is a rule, the parser can
replace beta by A, leaving the input alone. It accepts when the stack
is the start symbol S alone and only the end marker is left.</span>
</Formal>

<Examples of="bottom-up-parsing shift-reduce">

```text
greeting → "hello" name
name → "Ada" | "Alan"

shift hello     hello
shift Ada       hello Ada
reduce name     hello name
reduce greeting greeting  ✓
```


</Examples>

The first is the smallest parse there is: two shifts, then two
reductions, innermost first, ending with the start symbol alone. The
second is `1 - 2` under the subtraction grammar, written as the table

input on the right. The third is a moment in parsing `print (1 + 2);`
with the original adding grammar from the chapter on grammars, left
recursion and all: when the `)` arrives, `expr + item` is on top, the
parser reduces it to one `expr`, and it can then shift the `)` and
reduce `( expr )` to an `item`.

## Backwards, a rightmost derivation

Write the forms the parse went through from the bottom of the trace
to the top, joining stack and input, and something familiar appears.
For `1 - 2 - 3` they are:

```text
E
⇒ E - T
⇒ E - 3
⇒ E - T - 3
⇒ E - 2 - 3
⇒ T - 2 - 3
⇒ 1 - 2 - 3
```

Each line rewrites the *rightmost* nonterminal: it is a rightmost
derivation, read in reverse by the parser (the last reduction made is
the first step). That is the bottom-up counterpart of the last
chapter's fact that an LL parser finds a leftmost derivation; it is
also where the "R" in the name of the most important family of
bottom-up parsers, **LR** parsers, comes from: they read the input
from the **L**eft and produce a **R**ightmost derivation in reverse.
Donald Knuth defined them, and the grammars they can parse, in
1965<Cite id="knuth1965" />.

## Why left recursion is no trouble

Look again at the step where `E - T` became `E`. The parser used
`E → E "-" T` only after an `E`, a `-` and a `T` were all on the
stack; it never had to promise, before reading anything, that an `E`
was coming. The recursion on the left costs nothing, because by the
time the rule is used, its left-hand `E` is already finished. And
the tree comes out leaning left with no help: each new subtraction
wraps the `E` that is already on the stack, so `(1 - 2) - 3` falls
out of the grammar exactly as written. Grammars for bottom-up parsers
often prefer left recursion for a second reason: with
`list → list "," x`, each item is reduced as soon as it arrives, while
a right-recursive `list → x "," list` must shift the whole list before
it can reduce anything, so its stack grows with the list.

What we have not answered is how the parser *chooses*. In the
step-through the moves were given, and choosing well is not
obvious. With `E - T` on the stack, the top symbol `T` alone is also
the body of `E → T`, and reducing it would leave `E - E`, which no
rule can reduce:

```text
✗  E - T  ⇒ reduce E → T  ⇒  E - E  (stuck)
✓  E - T  ⇒ reduce E → E - T  ⇒  E
```

Which piece of the stack to reduce, and when, is the subject of the
next chapter.

## What you learned

- **Bottom-up parsing** builds the tree from the tokens up, and the
  root last.
- A **shift-reduce** parser keeps a stack: **shift** moves the next
  token onto it, **reduce** replaces a body on top with its head, and
  it accepts with the start symbol alone and the input used up.
- Its reductions, read backwards, are a rightmost derivation; LR
  parsers are named for that.
- Left-recursive rules work as written, and give left-leaning trees,
  because a rule is used only once its whole body has been seen.
