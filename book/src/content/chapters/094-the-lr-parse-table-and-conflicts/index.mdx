## From a picture to a table

The last chapter ended with a picture: nine states, joined by arrows
labelled with grammar symbols. A program cannot follow a picture, but
it can look things up; so we flatten the automaton into a table with
one row per state and one column per symbol, and the parser becomes a
loop that asks the table what to do. Here is the grammar again, with
its rules numbered (the numbers are how the table names them):

```text
0  S → E
1  E → E + T
2  E → T
3  T → n
4  T → ( E )
```

<Analogy title="a choose-your-own-adventure book">
Each page of the book says "if you open the door, turn to page 41; if
you run, turn to page 17". You never need to remember the story so far:
the page you are on already carries it, and the instructions on it are
all you need.
<span slot="breaks">In the book you only ever move forward to a new
page; an LR parser also goes *back*, popping pages off a pile after a
reduction and continuing from the page underneath.</span>
</Analogy>

The table has two halves. The **ACTION** half has a column for each
terminal, plus `$`, a marker the parser puts at the end of the input,
and each cell says *shift* (and which state to push), *reduce* (and by
which rule), *accept*, or, if empty, *error*. The **GOTO** half has a
column for each nonterminal and says which state to push after a
reduction has put that nonterminal on the stack:

```text
     n   +   (   )   $  |  E  T
0   s3  .   s4  .   .  |  1  2
1   .   s5  .   .   acc|  .  .
2   r2  r2  r2  r2  r2 |  .  .
3   r3  r3  r3  r3  r3 |  .  .
4   s3  .   s4  .   .  |  6  2
5   s3  .   s4  .   .  |  .  7
6   .   s5  .   s8  .  |  .  .
7   r1  r1  r1  r1  r1 |  .  .
8   r4  r4  r4  r4  r4 |  .  .
```

Every entry comes from one simple reading of the automaton. An arrow
on a terminal `a` from state `i` to state `j` gives *shift j* in row
`i`, column `a` (state 0 on `n` goes to 3, so `s3`); an arrow on a
nonterminal gives the GOTO entry (state 0 on `E` goes to 1); a complete
item `A → α ·` in state `i` gives *reduce* by that rule; and the item
`S → E ·` gives *accept* under `$`. The one surprising thing is the
rows full of `r2` or `r3`: an LR(0) item never looks at the next token,
so a reduction goes in **every** column of its row.

<Formal title="The LR(0) table">

```text
goto(Iᵢ, a) = Iⱼ, a terminal
    ⇒ ACTION[i, a] = shift j
goto(Iᵢ, A) = Iⱼ, A nonterminal
    ⇒ GOTO[i, A] = j
A → α · in Iᵢ, A ≠ S
    ⇒ ACTION[i, a] = reduce by A → α,
      for every terminal a
S → E · in Iᵢ
    ⇒ ACTION[i, $] = accept
```

<span slot="read">If goto from state i on a terminal a is state j, the
ACTION cell for i and a says shift j. If goto on a nonterminal A is
state j, the GOTO cell for i and A is j. If state i holds a complete
item for any rule but the start rule, every ACTION cell in row i says
reduce by that rule. If it holds the completed start rule, the cell
under the end marker says accept.</span>
</Formal>

## The parser is a loop

The stack now holds **state numbers**, not symbols; each state stands
for the symbol that led into it, so the symbols are only there for us
to read. One round of the loop: look up the row of the state on top and
the column of the next token. On *shift j*, push `j` and move to the
next token. On *reduce by A → α*, pop as many states as `α` has
symbols, which uncovers the state that was there before `α` was
started, and push `GOTO[that state, A]`. Stop on *accept*, or report an
error on an empty cell. Step through it on `n + n`:

## When a cell has two answers

Now make the grammar do a little more: add multiplication, which binds
tighter than `+` (chapter 83's precedence), with a new level `F` for a
factor:

```text
E → E + T     E → T
T → T * F     T → F
F → ( E )     F → n
```

Build its LR(0) automaton exactly as before and you get twelve states;
here is state 2, the state after a `T` at the start, with its row:

Bottom: a grammar whose state has two complete items gets two reductions in every cell.">

</Figure>

With `T` on the stack and `*` next, the parser cannot tell from the
table whether to reduce the `T` to an `E` now or to shift the `*` and
build a bigger `T` first. (Shifting is right, since `*` binds tighter;
but nothing in an LR(0) table says so.) State 9 has the same problem,
with `E → E + T ·` beside `T → T · * F`.

<Analogy title="a signpost with two arms">
You reach a fork where one sign says "Town, left" and the other says
"Town, right". Each is honest about one route, and you only have one
pair of legs.
<span slot="breaks">A walker can try one way and come back; a plain
LR parser has no going back, so a conflict is not a choice to make
later but a hole in the table that must be fixed before parsing.</span>
</Analogy>

<Callout kind="definition">
An **LR conflict** is an ACTION cell that would need two entries. In a
**shift/reduce conflict**, a state holds a complete item and an item
with a terminal after its dot, and the parser cannot tell whether to
reduce now or shift. In a **reduce/reduce conflict**, a state holds two
complete items, and the parser cannot tell which rule to reduce by. A
grammar is **LR(0)** when its LR(0) table has no conflicts.
</Callout>

<Examples of="lr-conflict">

```text
state 2:  E → T ·    T → T · * F
next "*": reduce by E → T, or shift *?
shift/reduce
```

```text
X → A    X → B    A → x    B → x
state:  A → x ·    B → x ·
reduce/reduce, in every column
```

```text
E → E + E    E → n      (ambiguous)
state:  E → E + E ·    E → E · + E
next "+": shift/reduce
```

</Examples>

The first is the one we met: a conflict that is only there because LR(0)
reduces without looking at the next token, and the next chapter makes it
disappear by looking. The second is the plainest reduce/reduce conflict:
after an `x` the parser has two complete items, and since `A` and `B`
are made of the same token, nothing it sees can separate them. The third
is the grammar of chapter 79's ambiguity: with `n + n` on the stack and
another `+` coming, reducing groups the sum as `(n + n) + n` and
shifting groups it as `n + (n + n)`; both are right by the grammar,
which is why no amount of lookahead fixes it. Some conflicts are
weaknesses of the method, and some are honest reports that the grammar
itself is ambiguous; telling them apart is most of the skill of writing
grammars for LR parsers.

<Formal title="When a grammar is LR(0)">

```text
for every state I:
  at most one complete item in I, and
  if A → α · is in I, no item in I
  has the form B → β · a γ, a terminal
```

<span slot="read">A grammar is LR(0) when, in every state, there is at
most one complete item, and a state with a complete item has no item
whose dot stands before a terminal. The first rule forbids
reduce/reduce conflicts and the second forbids shift/reduce
ones.</span>
</Formal>

<Callout kind="engine" label="Under the hood">
Knuth's 1965 paper framed the question this way: a grammar is LR(k)
when every parsing decision can be made with k tokens of
lookahead<Cite id="knuth1965" />. A conflict-free table is good news
beyond parsing, too: with exactly one possible move at every step, the
parser can only ever build one tree, so the grammar is not ambiguous. Almost no real
programming language is LR(0); the dragon book uses this same
`E`, `T`, `F` grammar to show why, and then adds lookahead<Cite id="aho2006" />,
which is the subject of the next chapter.
</Callout>

## What you learned

- The **ACTION** table says shift, reduce, accept or error for each
  state and terminal; the **GOTO** table says which state to push after
  a reduction. Both are read straight off the LR(0) automaton.
- The parser keeps **states** on its stack. A reduce pops one state per
  body symbol, then pushes `GOTO[uncovered state, head]`.
- An **LR conflict** is a cell with two actions: **shift/reduce** (a
  complete item beside a dot before a terminal) or **reduce/reduce**
  (two complete items). A grammar with none is LR(0).
