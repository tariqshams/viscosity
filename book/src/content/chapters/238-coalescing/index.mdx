## Copies, by luck or on purpose

When simplify and select coloured `sumsq` with four registers, `s0`,
`s1` and `s2` all happened to get the same register, and so did the
three versions of `i`; every `mv` became a copy of a register to
itself, which the back end simply deletes. Change the order in which
nodes are picked, and `s2` could as easily get a different register
from `s1`, leaving `mv s1, s2` in the loop body, one wasted
instruction on every trip. Leaving SSA creates a great many copies
(LLVM's phi elimination made six for `sumsq`), so the allocator
should not leave them to chance.

Recall the copy rule of the interference graph: a copy does not make
its source and destination interfere. So, unless something else
joins them, the two ends of a copy have no edge between them, and we
could decide in advance that they *will* share a register, by merging
the two nodes into one. The merged node interferes with everything
either of them interfered with, and the copy, now from a register to
itself, disappears. That is **coalescing**.

<Analogy title="one jar instead of two">
A kitchen has a jar of "flour" and a jar of "flour for the cake", and
you keep pouring from one into the other. If the two are never needed
separately at the same time, keep one jar, and the pouring stops.
<span slot="breaks">The single jar now has to fit in every place
either jar used to: whatever was in the way of either one is in the
way of the merged one, so a merged node has more neighbours, which is
exactly what can make the graph harder to colour.</span>
</Analogy>

<Analogy extra title="staying on the same train">
Changing trains at a junction costs time. If one train could run both
legs of your journey, you would stay in your seat and the change
would disappear.
<span slot="breaks">A train that runs both legs must keep to both
timetables, so it may clash with more trains than either did alone;
and, unlike a traveller, the compiler decides before anything runs,
for every possible journey at once.</span>
</Analogy>

<Callout kind="definition">
**Coalescing** merges the source and destination of a copy, two
values that do not interfere, into a single node of the interference
graph, whose neighbours are the neighbours of both. Both values then
get the same register, and the copy between them is deleted.
</Callout>

## Too much of a good thing

Chaitin's allocator coalesced every copy it could, as long as the two
ends did not interfere<Cite id="chaitin1982" />, which is now called
**aggressive** coalescing. The trouble is the breaks note above: the
merged node has the neighbours of both, and a node with many
neighbours is hard to colour. Here two values joined by a copy are
each easy to colour with two registers, but merged, they are not:

<Figure takeaway="Merging can turn two easy nodes into one hard one." caption="Left: with k = 2, the graph colours (a and b get different registers, so the copy stays). Right: merging a and b makes ab a neighbour of both x and y, which are neighbours of each other: a triangle, which two registers cannot colour. Removing one copy would cost a spill.">

</Figure>

A copy costs one cheap instruction; a spill costs loads and stores,
perhaps inside a loop. So we want **conservative** coalescing: merge
only when we can be sure the merge cannot make the graph harder to
colour by simplify and select.

## Two cautious tests

Both tests come from the same thought as simplify. A neighbour with
fewer than k neighbours of its own (a neighbour of *insignificant
degree*) will be removed by simplify anyway, so it can never be what
blocks a colouring. Only neighbours of degree k or more (*significant
degree*) are a threat.

The **Briggs test**: merge `a` and `b` only if the merged node would
have fewer than k neighbours of significant degree. Then, once
simplify has removed all the insignificant neighbours, the merged
node itself has fewer than k neighbours left and is removable too, so
the merge cannot cause a spill. (The test comes from the work of
Briggs, Cooper and Torczon<Cite id="briggs1994" />; George and Appel
gave it the name it has<Cite id="georgeappel1996" />.)

The **George test**, from George and Appel: merge `a` into `b` if
every neighbour of `a` either already interferes with `b`, or has
insignificant degree. The merged node's only new neighbours then are
ones simplify would remove anyway<Cite id="georgeappel1996" />. It is
one-sided (it looks only at the neighbours of `a`), which makes it
useful when `b` has a great many neighbours, such as a register fixed
by the calling convention.

<Formal title="The two conservative tests">

```text
Briggs(a, b):
  |{ t ∈ adj(a) ∪ adj(b) :
       deg(t) ≥ k }|  <  k
George(a → b):
  ∀ t ∈ adj(a):
       t ∈ adj(b)  ∨  deg(t) < k
```

<span slot="read">The Briggs test counts the neighbours t of a or b
whose degree (in the merged graph) is at least k, and allows the merge
if there are fewer than k of them. The George test allows merging a
into b if, for every neighbour t of a, either t is already a neighbour
of b, or t has fewer than k neighbours. (∀ reads "for every", ∨ reads
"or".)</span>
</Formal>

## Coalescing inside the allocator

George and Appel's **iterated register coalescing** puts the tests
inside the simplify loop rather than before it: simplify removes
nodes that are not part of any copy first, which lowers degrees, so
that more merges pass the conservative tests; it coalesces whenever a
test passes; and when neither can make progress, it **freezes** a copy
(gives up on merging it, so its nodes can be simplified like any
other) before it ever considers a spill<Cite id="georgeappel1996" />.
The same idea, correctness from copies and quality from coalescing,
is the second half of the out-of-SSA recipe in *Leaving
SSA*<Cite id="boissinot2009" />.

## What you learned

- **Coalescing** merges the two ends of a copy that do not interfere
  into one node, so they share a register and the copy disappears.
- **Aggressive** coalescing merges every such pair, and can turn a
  colourable graph into one that needs a spill, because the merged
  node has the neighbours of both.
- **Conservative** tests only allow safe merges: **Briggs** (fewer
  than k significant neighbours after merging) and **George** (every
  neighbour of `a` already touches `b` or has degree below k).
- **Iterated coalescing** interleaves simplify and coalescing, and
  freezes a copy before spilling.
