## Paying for the dead

Your Drip VM's collector (Part IV) does two walks: marking walks the
live objects, and sweeping walks *every* object, live or dead, to
unlink the dead ones. A program that makes a million short-lived lists
and keeps ten pays for a million in the sweep, which feels backwards:
the dead are exactly the objects nobody needs to look at again.

V8's Scavenger, in the chapter "V8's garbage collector", escapes that
cost by copying the survivors out, and you wrote a small version over
objects in a JavaScript array. This chapter goes down to the level a
real runtime works at: memory as a row of numbered **words** (the
fixed-size slots a machine reads in one go), objects as runs of words
and pointers as plain addresses. There, three questions become real:
where the copy goes, how the collector knows what it has already
copied, and how it keeps its to-do list without a stack.

## Copying collection

<Analogy title="repotting a plant">
You lift the living roots out with the soil clinging to them, set them
in a fresh pot, and tip the old pot's spent soil out in one go, without
picking through it.
<span slot="breaks">Roots are not tied to other plants. Objects point
at each other, so every pointer to a moved object must be rewritten to
its new address, which is most of the subtlety here.</span>
</Analogy>

<Analogy title="copying out a messy notebook">
A notebook full of crossed-out pages is easier to copy out fresh than
to tidy: you write the pages that still matter into a new notebook, one
after another, and bin the old one. The new notebook has no gaps.
<span slot="breaks">You can only copy into a notebook you already own,
so you must keep a spare one, the same size, blank and waiting; the
collector pays the same price in memory.</span>
</Analogy>

The heap is split into two halves of equal size, called
**semispaces**. The program allocates in one of them, *from-space*,
while the other, *to-space*, sits empty. When from-space fills, the
collector copies every object reachable from the roots into to-space,
packed one after another, and then the two swap names: the full,
abandoned half becomes the empty spare for next time.

<Figure takeaway="A copying collector touches only the live objects, and they end up packed together with no gaps." caption="Before and after one collection. Only the three reachable objects are copied; the garbage is never visited, and the whole of from-space becomes free at once.">
  <Semispaces />
</Figure>

Two good things fall out of that picture. Only live objects are
copied, so a collection costs what survives. And to-space ends up
**compacted**, the survivors side by side, so free memory is one
unbroken run and allocating means handing out the next address (V8:
after a scavenge, "new allocations happen at the next free
address"<Cite id="v8-trash-talk" />). The price is the spare half,
which is why the trick suits a small young generation far better than
a whole heap.

<Callout kind="definition">
A **copying collector** finds the live objects by tracing from the
roots, and copies each one into an empty area, rewriting every pointer
to point at the copy. The old area is then free in one piece. A
**semispace** collector divides the heap into two equal halves and
alternates between them. Its work is proportional to the live data,
and it compacts as it goes, but only half of its memory can hold
objects at any time<Cite id="gchandbook2012" />.
</Callout>

<Formal title="What each collector costs, roughly">

```text
copy  ≈ c₁ × L
sweep ≈ c₂ × L + c₃ × H
usable(copying) = H / 2
```

<span slot="read">With L live words and a heap of H words, copying
costs about a constant times L; mark-sweep costs a constant times L to
mark plus another times H to sweep; and a semispace collector can use
only half of H. These are models, not measurements: copying an object
is more work than marking it.</span>
</Formal>

<Examples of="copying-gc">

```text
heap: 1,000 objects, 10 reachable
mark-sweep visits 10, sweeps 1,000
copying copies 10, never sees 990
```

```text
let a = [];  let b = [a];  push(a, b);
a = none;    b = none;
# neither is reached: neither is copied
```

```text
Scavenge 4.4 (5.1) -> 3.5 (6.1) MB, …
  0.80 / 0.00 ms … allocation failure
```

</Examples>

The first is the whole argument in three lines: both collectors trace
the 10 live objects, but only mark-sweep goes on to visit the 990 dead
ones. The second is a pair of Drip lists that point at each other and
then lose their last outside reference; a reference-counting collector
(the chapter "Reference counting") needs extra machinery for such a
cycle, whereas a copying collector never reaches it, so it is never
copied, and its memory comes back with the rest of from-space. The
third is a real line from `node --trace-gc` on this book's machine
(Node v22.23.4, cut to fit): a scavenge took the young objects from
4.4 MB to 3.5 MB in 0.80 ms, and the reason at the end, `allocation
failure`, is the usual trigger for a copying collection: allocation
found no room left in the space<Cite id="v8-gc-tracer" />.

## Cheney's scan: a queue made of the copies

The obvious way to copy everything reachable is recursion: copy an
object, then copy what it points at. On a long chain that fails the way
the Drip marker would have without its grey stack:

<Run id="recursive-copy" />

The Drip marker fixed this with a pile of work. C. J. Cheney's
two-page paper of 1970 found something better: the copies themselves
can be the pile<Cite id="cheney1970" />. To-space is filled
from the front, so it already holds every object that has been copied,
in the order they were copied. Keep two pointers into it:

- **free**, the address where the next copy will go;
- **scan**, the first copied object whose fields have *not* yet been
  looked at.

Everything before `scan` is finished. Everything between `scan` and
`free` has been copied, but its fields still hold old from-space
addresses. The collector repeatedly takes the object at `scan`, copies
whatever its fields point at (each copy goes at `free`, pushing `free`
along), rewrites the fields to the new addresses, and moves `scan` past
it. When `scan` catches up with `free`, there is nothing left to do.

<Analogy title="a queue at a ticket office">
The people between the front of the queue and its end are waiting to
be served; each person served may phone friends, who join at the back.
The queue is empty when the person being served is the last one there.
<span slot="breaks">A ticket office forgets you once you are served.
To-space keeps everyone it has served, in order, since they are the
copied objects themselves; the queue is a window sliding along
them.</span>
</Analogy>

If those three regions sound familiar, they should: they are the three
colours of the chapter "Tracing and sweeping: white, grey and black".
Objects before `scan` are **black**, objects between `scan` and `free`
are **grey**, and objects still only in from-space are **white**. The
grey set is not a separate array; it is a stretch of to-space.

<CheneySteps client:visible />

<Callout kind="definition">
**Cheney's algorithm** copies the roots into to-space, then advances a
**scan** pointer through to-space, copying every object that a scanned
field points at to the **free** pointer and updating the field, until
`scan` equals `free`. Because the region between them is a first-in,
first-out queue, objects are copied **breadth-first**, and the
algorithm needs no recursion and no stack beyond its two
pointers<Cite id="cheney1970" />.
</Callout>

## Forwarding pointers

There is a hole in the plan: an object that two others point at must
be copied *once*, and both pointers must end up at the same copy. So
the moment an object is copied, the collector overwrites the old one
with a note: "moved; the copy is at address n". That note is a
**forwarding pointer**. Every later visit to the old address finds the
note instead of an object, and returns the new address without copying
again.

<Analogy title="a forwarding address at the post office">
When you move house you leave your new address with the post office;
letters sent to the old house are sent on, and nobody needs to know
you moved until they write.
<span slot="breaks">The post office keeps forwarding for months. The
collector needs the note only while it runs: once every pointer has
been rewritten, the whole of from-space is thrown away, notes and
all.</span>
</Analogy>

The note does two jobs at once. It is the "already copied" mark (the
Drip collector's `isMarked` bit in another form), so a shared object is
copied once and a cycle cannot loop for ever; and it maps the old
address to the new one, which is how a stale field gets fixed.
Overwriting the old object costs nothing, since nobody will read it as
an object again. V8's scavengers leave the same kind of note, so other
threads that reach the object can update their pointers
too<Cite id="v8-trash-talk" />.

<Examples of="cheney-scan forwarding-pointer">

```text
from: A@0 [2 | 7 3]   B@3 [1 | 9]
to after roots:  A@0 [2 | 7 3]
scan = 0, free = 3
```

```text
from[0] = 'fwd'  from[1] = 0
# A has moved to address 0 of to-space
```

```text
tree:  1 → 2, 3   2 → 4, 5   3 → 6, 7
to-space order: 1 2 3 4 5 6 7
```

</Examples>

The first is the start of the stepper: to-space holds an exact copy of
A, so its fields still say 7 and 3, from-space addresses; `scan` is at
A and `free` just past it. The second is our heap's forwarding
pointer: the header becomes the marker `'fwd'` and the next word the
new address (so in this toy heap every object has at least one field;
a real runtime picks its own place for the note). The third shows
breadth-first order on a tree: node 3 lands between node 2 and its
children 4 and 5, where depth-first would have kept 2, 4 and 5
together. Objects used together run faster when they share a cache
line (the chapter on spatial locality), so Cheney trades a little
locality for needing no stack.

## What you learned

- A **copying collector** carries live objects into an empty space and
  abandons the old one, so it costs what survives and compacts for
  free; a **semispace** collector pays for that with half its memory.
- **Cheney's scan** uses to-space itself as the queue: black before
  `scan`, grey between `scan` and `free`, white still in from-space; no
  recursion is needed, and the copy order is breadth-first.
- A **forwarding pointer**, written over the old copy, marks an object
  as moved and says where to, so shared objects and cycles are copied
  once and every pointer is rewritten to the same place.
