## Matching is not enough

The last chapter's shift-reduce parser followed orders it was given. A
real parser must make its own, and the obvious rule, "reduce whenever
a body is on top of the stack, else shift", goes wrong. Here it is on
`n + n * n` (`n` for a number) with the layered grammar of
*Grammars and parse trees*, its levels renamed `E`, `T` and `F`
(`E → E "+" T | T`, `T → T "*" F | F`, `F → n`), reducing the longest
body it can find:

<Run id="greedy" />

`greedy` checks every rule; `t = stack.slice(-b.length)` is the top of
the stack as long as the body, and the rule wins if those symbols are the
body (`same`) and it is longer than any found so far (`longer`). If a
rule wins, the body comes off and the head goes on; otherwise it
shifts; with nothing left to shift, it stops. It prints each stack,
and the trouble comes early: as soon as `E + T` is on the stack, it is
reduced to `E`, *before* the parser has looked at the `*`. The
multiplication then has nothing to attach to, and the parse ends
`stuck` with `E * E`.

`E + T` matched a body; however, it was the wrong thing to reduce at
that moment, because the `T` was not finished. The piece that is right
to reduce has a name.

## Handles

<Analogy title="sums in brackets">
To work out `(2 × (3 + 4)) − 1` by hand, you can't start with the `×`:
its right side is not a number yet. You first do the innermost bracket
that is complete, `3 + 4`, and only then the `×` around it.
<span slot="breaks">Brackets show you the innermost piece; a parser
has no brackets for `2 + 3 × 4`, and must work out from the grammar,
and the next token, which piece is complete.</span>
</Analogy>

<Analogy extra title="nesting dolls">
Packing nesting dolls, you close the smallest doll first, put it in
the next one and close that, and so on outwards; you can only close a
doll once everything that belongs inside it is already inside.
<span slot="breaks">Each doll has exactly one place to go; a grammar
symbol on the stack may later turn out to belong to a bigger piece
than it seemed, which is what the next token can tell.</span>
</Analogy>

<Callout kind="definition">
A **right sentential form** is one that appears in some rightmost
derivation. Its **handle** is the body of a rule `A → β`, at a
particular place in the form, such that replacing that β with `A`
gives the form one step earlier in the rightmost derivation. In a
shift-reduce parse, the handle is always at the top of the stack;
so the parser's only questions are whether the top is a handle yet,
and of which rule<Cite id="aho2006" />.
</Callout>

<Formal title="A handle">

```text
S ⇒*rm α A w  ⇒rm  α β w
       (w has only terminals)
then  A → β, just after α,
is a handle of  α β w
```

<span slot="read">The subscript rm means "rightmost": each step
rewrites the rightmost nonterminal. If the start symbol S becomes alpha
A w in zero or more rightmost steps, where w is all terminals, and one
more rightmost step, using A is made of beta, gives alpha beta w, then
that beta, just after alpha, is the handle of alpha beta w. Because w
is all tokens, the handle is always the last piece of the form before
the tokens the parser has not yet shifted: the top of the
stack.</span>
</Formal>

<Examples of="lr-handle">

```text
greeting → "hello" name
name → "Ada" | "Alan"
hello Ada    handle: Ada  (name → Ada)
hello name   handle: hello name
```

```text
E → E "-" T | T,  T → NUMBER
E - T - 3   handle: E - T
            (not T, by E → T)
```

```text
E → E "+" T | T
T → T "*" F | F,  F → NUMBER
stack E + T, next *: no handle
stack E + T * F:     T * F
```

</Examples>

The first is the smallest: `Ada` is reduced first, then the whole
form. In the second, `T` alone matches `E → T`, but reducing it would
give `E - E - 3`, which no rightmost derivation passes through; the
step back is to `E - 3`, so `E - T` is the handle. The third is the
greedy parser's mistake: with `*` next, `E + T` is not yet a handle,
because the `T` will grow into `T * F`; once that is on the stack,
`T * F` is the handle, and `E + T` becomes one after it.

## A table that finds handles

How can a parser know, without searching, whether the top of its stack
is a handle? The key fact, from Donald Knuth's 1965 paper that defined
LR parsing<Cite id="knuth1965" />, is that the stack contents that can
ever appear during a parse form a *regular language*, so a finite
automaton can keep track of them as symbols are pushed and
popped<Cite id="aho2006" />. An LR parser therefore keeps a stack of
that automaton's **states**, one above each symbol, and the state on
top summarises everything below it. A table, with two parts, says
what to do:

<Figure takeaway="Two lookups per step: ACTION to decide, GOTO after a reduction." caption="The LR table for E → E + T | T, T → T * F | F, F → n. Rules are numbered 1 to 5 in that order. ACTION cells hold sN (shift, then go to state N), rN (reduce by rule N) or acc (accept); an empty cell is a syntax error. GOTO cells say which state to go to after reducing to E, T or F. Row 7, the state for E + T, shifts on * and reduces on + and $.">
  <LRTable />
</Figure>

The **driver** that runs it is the same for every grammar. Look up
ACTION for the state on top and the next token. To shift, push the
state it names and move past the token. To reduce by a rule, pop one
state per symbol of its body, then push GOTO for the state now on top
and the rule's head. Accept when the table says so. Step through it:

<LRSteps client:visible />

Look at step 9. The stack holds `E + T`, exactly the body of rule 1;
however, the next token is `*`, and row 7 says shift. That one cell is
where `*` binds tighter than `+`: the table knows that, in this state,
a `*` means the `T` is still growing. In step 13, with `$` next, the
same row says reduce. The rules reduced, 5, 4, 2, 5, 4, 5, 3, 1, read
backwards, are the rightmost derivation of `n + n * n`, with the
product inside the sum.

<Callout kind="engine" label="Under the hood">
This driver loop is the whole run-time part of parsers made by tools
such as yacc and its descendants: the tool's real work is building the
table from the grammar, and the next two chapters do exactly that,
from items that record how much of each rule has been seen, to the
lookahead sets that decide each reduce<Cite id="aho2006" />.
</Callout>

## What you learned

- The **handle** of a right sentential form is the rule body whose
  reduction is one step back along the rightmost derivation; in a
  shift-reduce parse it is always on top of the stack.
- Matching a body is not enough: whether `E + T` is a handle depends
  on the next token.
- An **LR parser** keeps a stack of states; ACTION[state, token] says
  shift, reduce or accept, and GOTO[state, head] says where to go after
  a reduction. Precedence lives in the table's cells.
- The driver is the same for every grammar; building the table is the
  next chapter's work.
