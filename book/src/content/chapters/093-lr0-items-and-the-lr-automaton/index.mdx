## Is the top of the stack a handle?

The last chapter's parser works by **shifting** tokens onto a stack and
**reducing** whenever the top of the stack is a **handle**, the body of
a rule that should be replaced by its head right now, and it knew which
by looking in a table of states it was handed. That leaves one question
unanswered, and it is the whole question: where do those states come
from? Trying every rule against the stack after every token would be
slow, and would repeat work, since the stack changes one symbol at a
time.

A scanner had the same problem with characters, and "Finite automata"
solved it with a DFA, whose current state summarises everything read so far.
This chapter builds that kind of machine for a parser: its states
summarise the stack, so each shift or reduce costs one step instead of
a fresh look. We will build it for this grammar:

```text
S → E
E → E + T
E → T
T → n
T → ( E )
```

Here `E` is an expression, `T` a term, `n` stands for a number token,
and `+`, `(` and `)` are themselves; to keep things short, in these
chapters capital letters are nonterminals and everything else is a
terminal. The first rule is one we add on top of the grammar: `S → E`
gives the parser a single rule whose completion means "the whole input
was one expression; we are done".

## Items: a rule with a dot in it

<Analogy title="a bookmark in a recipe">
Halfway through a recipe you put a bookmark between "fold in the flour"
and "bake for twenty minutes". The bookmark says what you have done and
what comes next, without you having to remember any of it; and if you
are following two recipes at once, you have two bookmarks.
<span slot="breaks">A cook follows one recipe they chose; a parser
often keeps bookmarks in several rules at once, because it does not yet
know which one the input is following.</span>
</Analogy>

The parser's bookmark is written as a dot inside a rule. Every rule
gives one item for each place the dot can stand:

```text
E → · E + T    nothing seen yet
E → E · + T    seen an E; a + should come
E → E + · T    seen E +; a T should come
E → E + T ·    seen all of it: reduce
```

<Callout kind="definition">
An **LR(0) item** is a production rule with a dot somewhere in its body.
The symbols before the dot have been seen (they are on top of the
stack); the symbols after it are what the parser hopes to see next. An
item with the dot at the end is **complete**: its whole body is on the
stack, so it is a candidate for a reduction.
</Callout>

<Formal title="An item">

```text
A → α · β      where A → αβ is a rule
```

<span slot="read">A goes to alpha, dot, beta, where A goes to alpha
beta is a rule of the grammar. Alpha is the part already seen and beta
the part still to come; either may be empty. When beta is empty the
item is complete.</span>
</Formal>

<Examples of="lr0-item">

```text
T → · n     about to see a number
T → n ·     saw it: reduce n to T
```

```text
T → · ( E )   T → ( · E )
T → ( E · )   T → ( E ) ·
```

```text
after "( n +": inside a bracket,
E → E + · T   is where we are
```

</Examples>

A body of one symbol gives two items, before and after; a body of three
gives four, one for each gap. The third is the parser halfway through
`(1 + 2)`: it has shifted `(`, reduced the number to an `E` and shifted
the `+`, and the item says that what it needs next is a `T`.

## Closure and goto

Stand at `E → E + · T`. The parser wants a `T`, but no `T` arrives in
the input; numbers and brackets do. So, if we are waiting for a `T`, we
are also, at the same moment, at the very start of every rule for `T`:
`T → · n` and `T → · ( E )`. Adding those items, and repeating for any
new dot that lands in front of a nonterminal, is called **closure**.

<Analogy extra title="a to-do list that unfolds">
If the next line on your list is "get to the station", then the first
step of every way of getting there (walk to the bus stop, unlock the
bike) has quietly become something you might be about to do; and if
"unlock the bike" means "find the key", that joins the list too.
<span slot="breaks">You would pick one way and forget the others; a
parser keeps them all, because only the coming tokens can tell it
which way the input goes.</span>
</Analogy>

The second operation moves forward. **goto** of a set of items on a
symbol `X` takes every item with `X` right after its dot, moves the dot
over `X`, and closes the result: it answers "if `X` is the next thing on
the stack, where are we then?".

<Callout kind="definition">
The **closure** of a set of items: for every item with a dot in front
of a nonterminal `B`, add `B → · γ` for every rule `B → γ`; repeat until
nothing new is added. **goto(I, X)**: take the items of `I` of the form
`A → α · X β`, make them `A → α X · β`, and take the closure. If no item
has `X` after its dot, the result is empty.
</Callout>

<Formal title="closure and goto">

```text
B → γ in G,  A → α · B β in closure(I)
──────────────────────────────────────
      B → · γ in closure(I)

goto(I, X) = closure({ A → α X · β |
                 A → α · X β in I })
```

<span slot="read">If the grammar has a rule B goes to gamma, and the
closure already holds an item with the dot in front of B, then the
closure also holds B goes to dot gamma. And goto of I on X is the closure
of all the items of I that had X right after the dot, with the dot
moved over it.</span>
</Formal>

<Examples of="closure-goto">

```text
closure({ E → E + · T })
= E → E + · T,  T → · n,  T → · ( E )
```

```text
I = closure({ S → · E })
goto(I, n) = { T → n · }
goto(I, +) = { }   (no dot before +)
```

```text
goto(I, "(") = closure({ T → ( · E ) })
= T → ( · E ),  E → · E + T,  E → · T,
  T → · n,  T → · ( E )
```

</Examples>

In the first, the dot is before `T`, so both `T` rules join, and that
is all, since their dots stand before terminals, which add nothing. The
second starts from the closure of `S → · E` (five items, as the
step-through below shows): on `n` only `T → · n` moves, and on `+`
nothing does, so the answer is empty, which is a syntax error. The third
is closure working twice: the dot before `E` brings in the `E` rules,
and the dot in `E → · T` brings in the `T` rules. The left-recursive
rule `E → E + T` is added once and no more, since adding an item that
is already there changes nothing (so left recursion, the trouble of
"LL(1) tables and rewriting grammars", is no trouble here).

## The LR(0) automaton

Start from the closure of `S → · E`, call it state 0, and try goto on
every symbol that stands after a dot; each new item set is a new state,
and each item set we have seen before is an arrow back to an old one.
Keep going until nothing new appears. Step through it:

<Figure takeaway="The LR(0) automaton is a DFA whose states are item sets and whose arrows are goto." caption="The nine states found above. The arrow from nowhere marks state 0. Arrows on terminals happen when the parser shifts a token; arrows on nonterminals happen after a reduction puts that nonterminal on the stack.">

</Figure>

<Callout kind="definition">
The **LR(0) automaton** (also called the *canonical collection* of
LR(0) items) is the DFA whose start state is the closure of `S → · E`,
whose states are the item sets reachable from it by goto, and whose
transition on `X` from state `I` is `goto(I, X)`. It reads grammar
symbols, terminals and nonterminals alike, and a state with a complete
item `A → α ·` says "`α` is on top of the stack, and it may be a handle".
</Callout>

What it reads is the important part: the automaton does not read the
input, it reads the **stack**, from bottom to top. Run it over the
symbols on the stack, and the state you end in holds every item that
fits what is there.

<Examples of="lr-automaton">

```text
stack: n
0 -n-> 3   T → n ·   reduce n to T
```

```text
stack: ( E
0 -(-> 4 -E-> 6   T → ( E · ), E → E · + T
nothing complete: shift the next token
```

<Run id="walk-stack" />

</Examples>

The first is the first decision on any input that starts with a
number: state 3's only item is complete, so the number is a handle. In
the second, state 6 has no complete item, so the parser must shift (a
`)` or a `+`) before it can reduce. The runnable one holds the arrows as
data and walks a stack through them: `E + T` ends in state 7 (reduce),
`E +` in state 5 (keep going), and `+` finds no arrow at all, because no
valid program has a stack like that.

If this feels familiar, it is the subset construction of "From regular
expressions to fast scanners" again: an item is like one NFA state, closure follows the ε-moves, and
goto is one step on a symbol. (A real LR parser never reruns the machine
from the bottom: it keeps the state number beside each stack symbol, so
it only takes the last step again.)

<Callout kind="engine" label="Under the hood">
Donald Knuth defined LR(k) grammars in 1965: those a parser can handle
in one left-to-right pass, deciding each step with at most k tokens of
lookahead<Cite id="knuth1965" />. (The two letters are usually read as
Left-to-right and Rightmost derivation, because the reductions of a
bottom-up parse, read backwards, are a rightmost derivation.)
The automaton in this chapter is the k = 0 case, which is why its items
are called LR(0) items: nothing in them looks at the next token. The
dragon book's chapter 4 works through the same constructions, on a
slightly larger version of this grammar<Cite id="aho2006" />. The next
chapter turns the automaton into a table and shows where it breaks.
</Callout>

## What you learned

- An **LR(0) item** is a rule with a dot: what is before the dot is on
  the stack, what is after is still to come; a dot at the end means a
  possible reduction.
- **Closure** adds `B → · γ` for every nonterminal `B` just after a dot,
  until nothing changes; **goto(I, X)** moves the dot over `X` and takes
  the closure.
- The **LR(0) automaton** has item sets as states and goto as arrows;
  it reads the stack, and the state it ends in says whether the top is
  a handle.
